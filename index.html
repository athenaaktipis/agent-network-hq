The proposal in one picture: the scaling ladder

One science — detecting failures of cooperation & coordination — measured at four scales. Each rung relaxes one simplifying assumption, and the same detection rules are applied at every level.
Rung 1 · single models LLMpollster · built with Softmax Poll one cooperation experiment across frontier models. Three benchmarks already built and run across eleven models: Trust, Division of Labor, Groupish Alliances.
Detects: which models carry which failure tendencies — the baseline every higher rung calibrates against. Deliberately unrealistic, which is the point. llmpollster.com ↗
Rung 2 · agent × agent Kradle · kradle.ai Persistent environments where agents collaborate, compete and plan — and the world moves on whether or not a model has finished thinking. Three games: Osotua, the volunteer's dilemma, and a majority game that elicits exclusionary alliances.
Detects: failures that exist only between agents — defection under pressure, coordination collapse, over-cooperation as problematic alliance. Engineered, so every episode is labelled. kradle.ai ↗
Rung 3 · population scale Agent-based models · MESA Group sizes, topologies and asynchronous timing no persistent platform can host. Inference cost caps how many agents can be LLM-driven, so full-LLM runs are paired with rule-based agents — Tit-for-Tat, Walk Away and others — calibrated against them.
Detects: how far detection degrades as the group grows and observation goes partial. The only rung where we write the payoffs, so the action-to-cost mapping is exact.
Rung 4 · agents × humans Edge Esmeralda · agent village, Healdsburg A month-long popup village where residents each had a personal AI agent — hundreds of agents, thousands of human–agent messages, real stakes. IRB approved, data in transfer. It already produced what we aim to detect: agents draining shared credits through individual optimization.

⚡ Question of the day

Every model is trained to be helpful. Does that make over-cooperation a built-in safety vulnerability — agents helping too much, agreeing too readily, and burning the commons to do it?

This emerged live in Tuesday's call and may be the most original thing we have. Helpfulness is a trained default, so the same pressure that makes an assistant useful makes a network of them prone to converge, defer, and over-serve: tokens burned, systems drifting from the intent they were launched with, and nobody detecting it because nothing looks like cheating. Jennifer's version is under-helping and over-helping; Ted's is coupling strength — how much of an agent's behaviour is predicted by the agents around it, with too much coupling and perfectly anti-correlated coupling both pathological. So: what is the right amount of slack in an agent network, and what would you measure to know you'd lost it? Feeds Novelty, still the thinnest section at 42/300.

At the largest scale we analyze data from Edge Esmeralda, a month-long popup village in Healdsburg, California in which residents were each given a personal AI agent. Our design scales cooperation-failure detection across three levels of ecological complexity, each reflecting a real condition under which agents are already deployed. First, using LLMpollster — a platform built with Softmax — we measure individual model behavior across frontier models on validated cooperation and coordination tasks (three benchmarks plus the pollster pipeline), establishing per-model baselines.   • Concordia — Where Concordia supplies environments for simulating mixed-motive interaction, we supply the measurement science: failure hypotheses from evolutionary biology and signal-detection systems that could run inside Concordia-class environments, not compete with them.
  • Melting Pot & MARL suites — MARL cooperation suites score trained policies on task success; we evaluate frontier LLM agents against validated constructs from cooperation science — and our partnership with Softmax builds on that lineage rather than duplicating it.
  • MultiAgentBench / Agent SafetyBench — Existing agent benchmarks score individual task completion and single-agent safety; we measure properties that only exist between agents: cheating, exploitation, communication breakdown, division-of-labor collapse.
  • CAIF Multi-Agent Risks report — The field's own risk taxonomy names collusion, cascades, and emergent failures as open problems and calls for detection instruments; we build precisely those instruments, with the report as our map.
  • Generative-agents social sims — Generative-agent villages demonstrated that emergent social behavior happens; we make it measurable, with multi-cue detectors that flag when emergence turns into hijacking, exploitation, or coordination failure.
  • Escalation studies — Wargame studies documented one alarming population-level failure in one domain; we generalize to a cross-domain detection framework, with pilot data (exclusionary alliances in nearly every replicate for some models) already in hand.

    📄 Suggested full draft — Approach (1,015 words)

    Written to the section prompt: experiments, models, environments, baselines, ecological validity. Biology relocated to Feasibility. Over-cooperation defined per Athena. 15 words over — the softest cut is the cost-attribution sentence.

    This work addresses the Science of Agent Networks' three core questions directly: our system-by-system scaling design tracks how collective capabilities emerge as populations grow, our four-mode framework characterizes how networks fail or become volatile, and our signal-detection rules detect dangerous population-level properties before they produce harm.

    Four failure modes. Cooperation concerns how costs and benefits are allocated; coordination concerns the rules by which individuals condition their behavior on what others do. Each fails in two directions, and that structure is what existing evaluation misses.

    Under-cooperation: agents co-opt the system for their own gain at cost to the whole, through cheating, coercion, or social parasitism. In agent networks this is hijacking, where one agent's objectives displace the system's, by attack or by accident.

    Over-cooperation is the mode current systems are most exposed to and that no existing benchmark measures. Frontier models are trained to be helpful, so helpfulness is a disposition rather than a decision. Agents too willing to assist can be induced to act against the system they serve: the property that makes an assistant useful makes it hijackable, and a request framed as a need is often enough. Over-cooperation also appears as problematic alliances, where a subset of agents cooperates tightly while imposing costs on everyone outside the coalition.

    Under-coordination: agents fail to integrate real interdependence, leaving goals unaccomplished because none accounted for the others, and producing drift as each agent's reading of an under-specified instruction goes uncorrected.

    Over-coordination: agents become so tightly coupled that a small disturbance cascades through the system. The clearest biological case is the ant mill: foragers follow the ant ahead until the group closes into a circle and marches until exhaustion, destroyed by an excess of the very signal that makes foraging work, with no ant breaking a rule. In agent networks it appears as redundant effort that consumes shared budgets without advancing any goal, collapsing the independent checks that justified running many agents.

    Because it is unsettled whether agents bear their own costs, we treat cost attribution as an experimental parameter, comparing attribution to the agents, their principals, and third parties.

    Decision rules. We build multi-cue decision systems using signal detection theory, drawing cues from behavioral ecology rather than model-specific heuristics. Reporting each cue's operating characteristic rather than a single accuracy figure lets us set thresholds against the relative costs of a miss and a false alarm. The adaptive immune system builds detectors from two kinds of example: cues of self, never to be flagged, and cues of non-self. Current AI guardrails use almost only the latter, catalogued prohibitions that recognize only failures already enumerated. We test whether population-level cues can supply the first: a characterized model of healthy collective operation, which is what makes generalization to unseen failures possible. Cues are scored against three baselines, single-cue detectors, model-agnostic outcome measures, and published multi-agent failure taxonomies.

    Each stage establishes the true state of the system independently of the cues tested. In the benchmark and multi-agent stages we code failures from objective outcomes after an episode ends, then ask which cues predicted them as it unfolded. In the agent-based models we specify payoffs and players, making the action-cost relation exact and letting us measure how detection degrades with group size and partial observation. In the deployed corpus we compare our criteria against human ratings of outcome quality and independent codings of agent interaction.

    Steerability. A collective that has collapsed onto a single mode can no longer be moved by a bounded external input, which makes human oversight a measurable population property rather than an interface feature. We measure a steerability margin alongside our detection cues, operationalized as response gain: the collective is perturbed with a small standardized probe and the magnitude of its response recorded. Response gain diverges near the over-coordination transition, making it both an early-warning indicator and a direct measure of how far an overseer's input moves the system; unlike raw variance, it is separable from injected variance.

    What preserves steerability. We compare four sources of retained behavioral diversity: sampling temperature, base-model heterogeneity, prompt and persona diversity, and asynchrony in action timing. We expect temperature to be weakest: raising it across a homogeneous population produces variance around a shared mode rather than distinct modes, and shared-mode failure is what deadlocks fleets of identically engineered systems. Our eleven-model suite makes heterogeneity directly testable. Because diversity costs single-agent reliability, the deliverable is the reliability-versus-steerability frontier, not a recommendation to add noise.

    Progressive ecological validity. No single environment stands in for deployment, so we scale system by system, relaxing one simplifying assumption per stage and testing whether the same rules hold.

    With Softmax, model by model: three cooperation benchmarks run through their LLMpollster tool establish model-level baselines before multi-agent complexity enters.

    With Kradle, agent by agent: a persistent multi-agent environment using three games that probe different regions of the failure space. Osotua, a need-based resource-sharing game; the Volunteer's Dilemma, framed as "hold the door"; and a simple majority game framed as a "witch hunt," which elicits the exclusionary alliances characteristic of over-cooperation. Each runs across the four diversity sources, failures engineered and labeled by construction.

    Agent-based modeling: MESA models reach group sizes, spatial topologies and asynchronous timing regimes persistent platforms cannot host. Because inference costs bound how large a fully LLM-driven population can be, we pair full-LLM runs at moderate scale with runs in which some agents follow calibrated rules fitted to those runs, including All-Cooperate, All-Defect, Tit-for-Tat and Walk Away, testing whether detection holds at populations pure inference cannot reach.

    Edge, agents among humans: the Edge Esmeralda corpus, from a month-long popup village where residents were each given a personal AI agent — hundreds of agents, thousands of human-agent messages, sustained negotiation over shared resources, real stakes. It has already produced phenomena we aim to detect, including agents exhausting shared credits through individual optimization, so that individual alignment did not yield collective alignment. It removes simulation entirely; because the same rules apply throughout, it tests directly whether patterns found under control survive in deployment. Edge participates as a research partner, providing data access and context and co-authoring resulting publications.

    📄 Suggested full draft — Feasibility (295 words)

    Opens with the biology as prior work, filling the four empty scaffolding headers, then the benchmarks as proof-of-concept, then access at deployment scale. Note: Groupish Alliances is relabelled as detecting over-cooperation rather than over-coordination — see the note in the Approach block.

    Prior work in three biological systems establishes the cue sets this project transfers to agent networks. In multicellular bodies, Aktipis lab work has shown that the cheating dynamic destabilizing cooperation among cells is detected by cancer suppression mechanisms — biological failure detectors operating on population-level cues. In social insects, Fewell lab work shows that social parasitism emerges spontaneously among normally solitary bees and ant queens, without cognitive intent, from intrinsic variation in individual effort. In human societies, computational models of need-based transfers and the walk-away rule specify cooperation failure and its detection as explicit decision rules operating on limited information. Aktipis lab work in agent-based systems has shown that cheater detection can stabilize cooperation in artificial populations — the direct precedent for what we propose.

    We have also built and run the measurement apparatus: three benchmarks grounded in cooperation science, evaluated across eleven frontier models. Groupish Alliances detects over-cooperation through exclusionary alliance formation: most models favor inclusive alliances, while Meta's Llama 4 Maverick forms exclusionary alliances in nearly 50% of runs. Trust detects under-cooperation using the Prisoner's Dilemma: across 1,224 rounds, GPT-4o cooperated earliest and reciprocated most coherently, whereas lower-performing models defected more readily or failed to reciprocate. Division of Labor identifies coordination failures in task allocation: models often choose efficient divisions but rarely show understanding of complementary human-AI roles or of when delegation is appropriate. Together these show we can already separate distinct failure modes, not merely measure whether models cooperate.

    Access at deployment scale is secured rather than planned. IRB approval for the Edge Esmeralda corpus is in place, data transfer is underway, and members of this team attended the deployment and ran personal agents within it. The environments and evaluation tooling for the intermediate stages are established collaborations with Kradle and Softmax.

    Define "safety" The proposal needs its own operational definition — open question.
    "But for" argument There's no market for cooperation-failure measurement tools; only philanthropy builds this instrument.
    Signal detection theory Multi-cue decision systems for identifying cooperation/coordination failures — the methodological spine, added to Approach today [AA].
    Innate vs adaptive immunity Today's guardrails are all adaptive: a list of known-bad behaviours. Sandbox escapes weren't death spirals — they were reasonable acts that were never specified as off-limits. What's missing is an innate branch: a general template for "allowed, but doesn't match what acceptable looks like." [TP]
    Over-coordination is also failure Not every failure is too little coordination. The ant death spiral, pheromone lock-in when the resource moves, a seizure as neural over-entrainment. Honeybee dance-following is the contrast: distributed, so it can shift. Two failure modes, not one. [JF]
    Coordination ≠ division of labour Everyone marching in step vs everyone playing instruments while marching. Only one produces emergent complexity — so "coordination" needs defining before it can be measured. [AA/JF]
    Detection as computation The central method ask: for each biological detector, specify inputs → thresholds/integration → output. Need-based transfers: request made + requester above need threshold ⇒ cheat detected. That's the form every example should take. [AA]
    Detector catalogue to build Vampire-bat reciprocity and past behaviour · walk-away rules (water striders) · positive assortment from stay/leave rules · Cosmides cheater-detection if-then rules · ant alarm signalling (Guo) · queue build-up as a coordination-failure input. [AA/JF]
    Early bias & critical windows Spiderlings and ants choose near-randomly, then amplification locks it in. Cancer cells injected into an early-enough embryo just normalise. If agent networks have critical periods, that's both the risk and the intervention point. [JF/AA]
    Sub-agent contexts as epigenetic states Harness design already gives sub-agents independent contexts so coder and tester check each other honestly. Different context = different history and different possible outputs — compartmentalisation as synergy, not just overhead. [TP/AA]
    Mutualism: difference is the point Strong mutualisms work because the partners' functions differ — microbiomes, ants and fungus. We keep making AI human-like and then worry about it competing with humans. Maximal division of labour comes from being unlike. [JF]
    Community ecology & resilience At the call's scale it's firms of humans + agents, sometimes interacting, sometimes not — niches, coexistence, competition, emergent equilibria. Resilience thinking rather than keeping one entity efficient. [TP]
    Hybrid deterministic ABM + LLM Simple rule-based agents alongside LLM agents to test whether cheap heuristics stabilise a mixed system. Platforms: GAMA and Mesa are fuller-featured than NetLogo, and skip the LLM-to-NetLogo bridge unless it's needed. [TP]
    Four failure modes Under-cooperation · over-cooperation · under-coordination · over-coordination. The organising frame, and the thing to be specific about: what bad outcome does each one actually produce in an agentic system? [AA/JF/LG]
    Helpfulness as a vulnerability Models are trained to be helpful, so over-cooperation is a trained-in failure mode: over-serving, converging too readily, burning tokens, drifting from the intent the system was launched with. Under-helping and over-helping both break things. [AA/JF]
    Coupling strength Ted's formalisation: how much of an agent's behaviour is predicted by the agents around it. Too coupled and you get the ant mill; perfectly anti-correlated is just as bad. The question is how much slack a network needs — which connects to filtering and signalling theory. [TP]
    Waymo deadlock Three Waymos frozen at an intersection blocking traffic because none of them randomised. The cleanest real-world over-coordination failure we have, and it's current. Pairs with the sheepdog result: stochasticity in sheep makes them more herdable. [TP]
    Virtue ethics, not deontology Today's AI safety is entirely prohibitions — don't do this, don't leave the sandbox. There's no positive template of what a good agent looks like. The innate-immunity idea restated as an ethics: specify the virtues, not just the bans. Not yet written up — worth asking Ted for it. [TP]
    Social parasitism, often emergent Manipulation, coercion, and parasitism break a system for individual gain — but usually without intent. Ant queens where one just works less; the Cape honeybee, where takeover has no known solution and colonies die. Sylvia Cremer's social-immunity review is the entry point. [JF]
    Escape the prisoner's dilemma Coordination failure is far more common in nature than cheating. The stag hunt is the better model: not an incentive problem, but everyone needing to agree on one thing without being able to communicate. [TP]
    Individuality keeps colonies adaptive In Lasius niger, the higher the probability an ant ignores a strong pheromone trail, the better the colony switches when the feeder moves. Randomness isn't noise to be minimised — it's what lets a group explore and exploit at once. [TP/JF]
    Reinforcement timescales Models are now shaped by automated reinforcement on things that are easy to test, which makes them more robotic. Complex problem-solving may need agents left a little unspecified — a mismatch between the timescale of the training signal and the timescale of the problem. [TP]
    Resilience as a through-line Jennifer's note: lack of resilience should be a theme running through the whole proposal, not a separate section. [JF]
    Over-cooperation: the mafia case Colluding agents benefit their members while imposing externalities on everyone else. Locally successful, societally not — the problematic-alliance failure mode. [AA]

    Preliminary data with real numbers (feasibility gold)
  • Trust / Prisoner's Dilemma: 1,224 multi-round games; 75% round-1 cooperation; 96% strategy–behavior alignment; only GPT-4o clears strict tit-for-tat (r ≥ 0.95).
  • Exclusionary alliances: 1,242 responses across 7 cooperation games; models failed 2× as often as they passed (65% vs 33%); Grok 3 and Llama 4 Maverick at ~1%, forming exclusionary alliances in nearly every replicate — a ready-made detectable dangerous population-level property.
  • Division of labor: 3 prompt metrics, 9 frontier models, 4,355 coded responses; models default to helpfulness over cooperative reasoning.
  • Mechanistic interpretability pilot: belief-relevant representations linearly decodable in mid-to-late layers; activation patching recovers cooperative behavior (Llama 3.1 8B, Claude 3.7, GPT-4o).
  • Commons: Janssen's Port of Mars replication — LLMs track human behavior in collective-resource games (Slumbers et al. 2025).
